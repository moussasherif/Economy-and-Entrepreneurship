Choosing the best course of action

Engineering economy is a toolkit for one question: of the projects or options on the table, which one is worth doing? The answer is only as good as the data behind it.

How to choose

Every decision has three parts: the focus, the list of alternatives, and a definition of “best”. The focus usually defines “best”.

Getting to class: walk, bus, or car. If “best” means cheapest, you walk. If it means fastest, you drive. Same alternatives, different answer, because “best” changed.

Multi‑factor problems

Real decisions mix monetary factors (easy to put in dollars) with non‑monetary or intangible ones (weather, safety, prestige). Two problems appear immediately: how to weight them, and how to compare different units.

Floor cleaning machine. Machine A costs $23,500/year with safety 7/10. Machine B costs $28,400/year with safety 8.5/10. A business that uses one criterion, cost, picks A. That simplicity is why this course works mostly with money.

Where the cost of a product comes from

Before comparing projects you need to know what things cost. Cost accounting splits a product’s cost into pieces you can trace, plus a pool of shared costs you spread out fairly.

The three building blocks

Total cost = Direct material + Direct labor + Overhead

This works per year or per unit.

Baking a cake to sell: flour and eggs are direct material, the baker’s hours are direct labor, and the oven’s electricity, the shop rent and the cashier are overhead. You can’t point at a slice and say how much rent is “in” it, so you need a rule to share it out.
CategoryWhat it isExamples
Direct material (DM)Principal materials that end up in the product. Small items (glue, screws) go to overhead.Wood for a table
Direct labor (DL)Labor charged directly to the productWages, overtime, social security tax, vacation pay, health and life insurance, pension
Overhead (OH, indirect)Every production expense that isn’t DM or DLDepreciation, maintenance, taxes, insurance, rent, interest on capital, sales, admin, research

Allocating overhead

Pick a resource every product uses (DM dollars, DL dollars, labor hours, machine hours, floor area…), then prorate:

OH rate = Total OH ÷ Total of chosen base

OH per unit = OH rate × base used by one unit
Furniture company. Total OH = $527,165, total DM = $328,500 → rate = $1.60 per $1 of DM.
Table: DM 40, DL 55, OH = 40 × 1.60 = 64 → TC = $159. Sold at $189 → profit = 189 − 159 = $30 per table (the slide prints $40, but the subtraction gives $30).

Fixed, variable, and break‑even

Another way to split cost: fixed F (doesn’t change with output, like rent) and variable V per unit (material per unit). For n units:

TC = F + nV · TR = n × price · Break‑even n* = F ÷ (price − V)

Defaults are the seminar example. Try F = 10,000, V = 10, price = 15 → 2000 units.

A pound today is not a pound next year

Interest raises the value of money over time; inflation lowers what it can buy (handled separately). Everything in this chapter is about moving money along a timeline so amounts at different times can be compared.

Simple vs compound interest

Simple: interest only on the principal. I = P·i·N. $2,000 at 12% for 5 years → I = 1,200, total 3,200.

Compound: interest on principal and on past interest. Always assume compound unless told otherwise.

The factors

Read (F/P, i, n) as “find F, given P”. The letters cancel like units: P × (F/P) = F.

Think of a factor as an exchange rate between two points in time. (F/P, 8%, 24) = 6.341 means “1 pound today trades for 6.341 pounds in year 24”, just as an exchange rate converts EGP to USD.

Where the series factors drop their result

  • P from a series lands one period before the first A.
  • F from a series lands on the last A.
  • If a series doesn’t start at year 1, move it in two steps: series → single amount → wherever you need it. This is the trick behind most sheet problems.

Recipe for any problem

  1. Read the problem and draw the cash flow diagram.
  2. Label what you know and what you want using only P, F, A (and G).
  3. Pick a common point in time. Two cash flow sets are equivalent if their values are equal at the same point.
  4. Write the equation with factors, look each one up in the tables, and solve. Split the diagram into simple pieces and add them if needed.

Reading the compound interest tables

Each page of Appendix B is one interest rate. Every row is a number of periods n. Every column is one factor. Find three things and you have your number.

The three‑step lookup

  1. Find the page for your interest rate i, printed at the top (e.g. “8% Compound Interest Factors”).
  2. Find the row for n in the left (or right) column.
  3. Find the column for the factor. Columns are grouped: Single Payment (F/P, P/F), Uniform Payment Series (A/F, A/P, F/A, P/A), Arithmetic Gradient (A/G, P/G). Each header also says it in words: “Find F Given P”.

Factor finder

This rebuilds the table row for any i and n, highlights the cell, and interpolates when the rate isn’t printed.

When the rate isn’t in the tables: interpolate

Sheet 1 uses 16%, 14% and 5.5%, none of which have a page. Take the two closest printed rates and assume a straight line between them.

factor ≈ flow + (i − ilow) / (ihigh − ilow) × (fhigh − flow)
It’s like reading a ruler between two marks: if 16 sits one‑third of the way from 15 to 18, the factor sits one‑third of the way between their two values. The real curve bends slightly, so the result is close but not exact; the formula gives the exact value.

The same idea works in reverse when you’re solving for i (Sheet 2 Q10): find the factor value you need, see which two rates bracket it, and interpolate for i.

Quick checks that catch mistakes

  • (P/F) = 1 ÷ (F/P), (P/A) = 1 ÷ (A/P), (F/A) = 1 ÷ (A/F)
  • (A/P) = (A/F) + i. At 10%, n=10: .1627 = .0627 + .10
  • F/P and F/A are always above 1 (for n ≥ 1). P/F is always below 1. If a present value comes out bigger than the future amount, a factor is upside down.
  • For very long horizons (n → ∞): (P/A) = 1/i, (A/P) = i, (P/G) = 1/i², (A/G) = 1/i.
  • Small differences between the table and the formula (like 76,092 vs 76,094) are just rounding in the table. Both are accepted.
